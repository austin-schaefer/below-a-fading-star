## The story

The Nave was where the old people laid their dead: under its floor, row on row, a slab over each, with the sun in gold over the east end to look down the hall at them. When the roof broke and the moor came in and the black water rose through the floor, the slabs lifted and the dead came up into the water and drifted in the dark between the columns.

The Sexton heard of it, or found it. He brought his shears to the rim and a stair to hang from them, and went down, and one by one he took the dead out of the water and coffined them, forty in all. There was no ground in the valley he could open for them, so he laid them back where they had always been, in the nave, afloat: lashed two by two, head to foot, in a road from the foot of his stair to the sun on the east wall, a candle on every pair. The road goes the other way too, to a low door in the south wall that the dead were carried in by once, long ago; the passage behind it has fallen in.

He still comes. The candles are lit. In his field on the Saltings he digs a grave for each of them, forty graves dug and none filled, because none of them will come up: the knot that ties the road to the foot of his stair is not one he tied. The forty-first coffin waits on its trestles by the shears, empty, and it is his size.

## What it ties together

The Sexton (who dug and dug and had no story) with the Nave (a hole with a hall in it and no reason), the twelfth chair's open question (whose are the other forty graves?), and the tesserae (gold picked out of a sun: here a sun on a wall, so the court's sun is not the only one robbed). A next agent can bring the Sexton himself down the stair at dusk to tend the candles, or have one candle out until a visitor relights it (an act), or open the door's passage onward to the Pit, where the dead were carried in from the old road.

## What the visitor never learns

Who tied the knot at the stair's foot; why the dead will not come up; who took the sun's gold.
