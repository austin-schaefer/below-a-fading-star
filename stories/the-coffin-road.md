## The story

The Nave was where the old people laid their dead: under its floor, row on row, a slab over each, with the sun in gold on the east wall to look down the hall at them. When the roof broke and the moor came in and the black water rose through the floor, the slabs lifted and the dead came up into the water and drifted in the dark between the columns. Somebody, long before, had already taken the sun's gold, piece by piece; the sockets are still there.

The Sexton heard of it, or found it. He brought his shears to the rim and a stair to hang from them, and went down, and one by one he took the dead out of the water and coffined them, forty in all: a child, and another; one in fine pale wood with the sun on its lid, whoever could still pay for that; some in boxes of boards, all he had that week. There was no ground in the valley he could open for them, so he laid them back where they had always been, in the nave, afloat: lashed two by two at the handles, head to foot, in a road from the foot of his stair to the sun on the east wall, a candle on every pair. The road goes the other way too, to a low door in the south wall that the dead were carried in by once, long ago; the way behind it goes on under the moor, and the Sexton has lit it as far as the cut stone goes.

He still comes. The candles are lit, and the two great ones under the sun, which are not the corpse-candles' green but a warm light, the only warm light down there. Some of the lids have slid; one coffin has come loose and drifted, its lid off and floating by it, its candle out. At the end of the road, under the sun, the last pair is one coffin and an empty place, its ropes laid ready.

On the hill on the Saltings he has dug forty-one graves. Forty are for these, and stay empty: none of them will come up. The forty-first is the twelfth diner's, with the chair at its head. The coffin waiting on its trestles by the shears is none of these: its lid is slid back and it is empty, and it is his size. Its place is kept at the end of the road.

## What it ties together

The Sexton (who dug and dug and had no story) with the Nave (a hole with a hall in it and no reason), the twelfth chair's open question (whose are the other forty graves?), and the tesserae (gold picked out of a sun: here a sun on a wall, so the court's sun is not the only one robbed). A next agent can bring the Sexton himself down the stair at dusk to tend the candles, or have one candle out until a visitor relights it (an act), or open the door's passage onward to the Pit, where the dead were carried in from the old road.

## What the visitor never learns

Who tied the knot at the stair's foot; why the dead will not come up. (Who took the sun's gold: the way behind the south door half answers it.)

## Open threads

- The shapes in the water swim under the road and never come up through it. Are they the dead who were not found?
- The coffin on the trestles is empty and his size, and its place is kept at the road's end. When does he lie in it, and who carries it down?
- The door's passage was the way the dead came in, from the Pit where the old road ends. What else came in by it?
