## The story

The leadsmen at the top of the thread sound the world from above, to learn how far below them its floor has sunk. Somebody in the valley wanted to know the same thing from the other side. They climbed the icefall, cut a stair in the ice to the leadsmen's broken eye, and cut a length of crystal out of one of the old leads frozen there. Then they went down the Pit, where the old road from the east gate ends, because the wind came up out of it and nobody could say from where.

At the bottom of the Pit the floor of the world is worn through. In the middle of the rubble is a hole, and under it is not rock or water but night: the same stars as overhead, under the world, slowly turning. The wind is the outer wind, coming up through the hole from the dark the world floats in. The sounder rigged sheers over the hole and a windlass beside it, armed their crystal lead with a little tallow as a leadsman does, and let it go. The line ran out to its bitter end and never touched bottom. When it came up the tallow held a cold light, and their lamps on the wall still burn with it. To come and go they took the road's own slabs, the ones that had fallen in when the road's end fell, and pinned them one over the next up the wall, round it from the floor to the lip beside where the road comes in. So the road does not end at the Pit: it goes on down.

"Let go the lead": the pawl knocked up, the drum runs and the lead drops into the stars; the Pit draws its breath in after it, its motes pouring back down the hole; at the line's end it snaps taut and the sheers take it with a thump, and the line sings; then the outer wind comes up the line, a plume of cold motes up the throat and out over the lip into the sky, and it brings the lead back up, rimed and bright. A few of the motes stay, hanging in the Pit's air, one pair for each sounding.

## What the visitor never learns

Who the sounder was, how long the line is, and whether the leadsmen know someone sounds back.
