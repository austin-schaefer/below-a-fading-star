## The story

The Heliarchs, who built facing the sun, cut a way from their hall under the moor to the well where the old road ends: a stair down from the hall's south door, a round room on the way with a bench round its wall, a bridge on one arch over a crack in the world, and a door into the well's side with their sun carved over it. The wind that comes up the well from under the world found the way and took it: it scoured the stretch past the room round and smooth and fluted, and it still blows up it, carrying a rime of cold light that clings to the rock.

The people after them stopped facing the sun and tried to keep it: they gathered daylight while there was daylight and sent it by tube. One of their lines ran through the drowned hall and on down the Heliarchs' stair toward the well, a tube made in one piece, ribbed and seamless, milk-white, with a swelling wherever it levelled. It broke where the wind's bore begins. Two of their carriers are still in it, sealed vessels of bottle amber, stalled where they stopped; a few of its lenses still hold a little stored daylight, ebbing.

Somebody has been picking the gold out of every sun in the valley: the one in the Sunken Court's floor, the one on the Nave's east wall. It comes here. In the Heliarchs' round room the Reckoner is laying a new sun into the floor, piece by piece, by the light of his own lamp, under the ground where no gutter reaches it, against the day the one in the sky does not come back. The middle is laid and five rays, and half a sixth; the rest is drawn out in chalk. Round its rim he has cut his eleven glyphs and filled the first three with gold. His pad is where he kneels; his trowel lies by it, his basket of gold beside the bed (a few red and ochre pieces among the gold, prised off the Nave's rays by mistake, and one grey), and his wire with a bead on it for every ray he has laid. His lamp is lit. He is never there when you are.

The sounder hung their lamps of cold light from the Pit's door up the stair, over the bridge and up the bore, to the room's door, and no further: it is someone else's. The one warm lamp by the Pit's door is the Reckoner's: it is the way he comes.

## What it ties together

The Reckoner's question ("Two floors, or one?") gets a third floor for an answer, and the gold that goes missing from the valley's suns gets somewhere to go. Three peoples' work is laid one on another along one way (the Heliarchs' cut stone at the Pit's end and in the room, the fourth people's tube at the Nave's, the wind's own bore between), and three lights with three owners mark it: stored daylight, failing, from the Nave's end; the Reckoner's warm lamp in his room; the sounder's cold lamps from the Pit. A next agent could have the sun's last ray grow by a few pieces at every gutter, or put a piece of the Nave's red in the jackdaw's nest, or bring the Reckoner's lamp up the Hypogeum's stair and on, far below, to this room.

## What the visitor never learns

Whether the Reckoner's stair below his door comes out here; why he lays it where nobody will see it shine; what he will do when the gold runs out; what the stalled carriers hold.

## Open threads

- The sun's last ray is half laid. Does it grow, and with what gold, once the valley's suns are bare?
- The sounder's lamps stop at the room's door, as if they knew whose it was. Have the sounder and the Reckoner met?
- Two carriers stalled in the Line, sealed. Is there daylight in them still, and would the Reckoner want it for his sun?
