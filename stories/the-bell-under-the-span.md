## The story

The Heliarchy cast a bell for the Span and hung it from the crown of one bay, and the bell outlasted them. Its weight split the crown over the ages: the crack a running hop clears on the deck is the bell's doing, and its chains go up into it still, over a timber someone wedged across. It hangs eight metres off the ground in the vault's shadow, too big to ring from inside, green with age, a crack of its own up from the lip.

The Late folk could not ring it the old way, so they slung a ram beside it, a trimmed trunk with a great iron head, tied their saffron rags round it, and ran its rope over a sheave on the pier and down to a ring on a stone at the pier's foot. In this country a bell is rung for the dead, and the dead are here: the traveller lies on the slope beyond the bell's mouth where he came down, unburied, years gone, his letters blown across the grass from his burst case. When the bell is rung, the stroke runs out over the low ground south of the Span as a wave of dust, and where it reaches the corpse-candles kindle, one after another, and come in toward the bell, and gather in its mouth (it hums to them, and fills with their light), and wait there for the ringer; then they lead out through the arch to him and stand round him. Gather his letters and lay them on his chest, and they are done: they go up past the Span and out among the stars, all but one, which stays over his head.

## What it ties together

The crack on the deck (a thing to jump) and the bell (a thing to ring) are one thing; the traveller's open thread ("Beat 2 needs something that can be seen from sixty metres up") is answered by the candles; the fen's wisps are given a will. The ram's saffron rags are the Late folk's colour, as at the Lantern Halt.

## What the visitor never learns

Who sent the candles, or whether they are the fen's dead come to keep one of their own.
