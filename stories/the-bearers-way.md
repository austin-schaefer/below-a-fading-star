## The story

The old people who laid their dead in the Nave carried them in from where the old road ends, at the well that is the Pit now. Their way went down a cut stair under the moor, and for a stretch through a round bore that was not theirs: the wind that comes up from under the world had made it, and still uses it. Halfway they cut a round room with a bench round its wall, where the bearers set the dead down and sat. Then a stair over a crack in the world, on one arch, and the last flight down to a door in the well's side with a sun carved over it. When the Nave foundered and the road's end fell in, nobody came that way any more, and the stair at the Nave's end fell in on itself.

Somebody has been picking the gold out of every sun in the valley: the one in the Sunken Court's floor, the one on the Nave's east wall. It comes here. In the bearers' room the Reckoner is laying a new sun into the floor, piece by piece, by the light of his own lamp, under the ground where no gutter reaches it, against the day the one in the sky does not come back. The middle is laid and five rays, and half a sixth; the rest is drawn out in chalk. Round its rim he has cut his eleven glyphs, and filled the first three with gold. His pad is where he kneels; his trowel lies by it, his basket of gold beside the bed (a few red and ochre pieces among the gold, prised off the Nave's rays by mistake, and one grey), and his wire with a bead on it for every ray he has laid. His lamp is lit. He is never there when you are.

The others stop short of his room. The Sexton lit his candles down the stair from the door as far as the cut stone goes; where the bore begins the draught has put the last one out. The sounder hung their lamps of cold light from the Pit's door up the stair, over the bridge and up the bore, to the room's door, and no further.

## What it ties together

The Reckoner's question on the Nave's story ("Two floors, or one?") gets a third floor for an answer, and the gold that goes missing from the valley's suns (the jackdaw, the Nave's east wall) gets somewhere to go. The Coffin Road's door, the Pit's floor and the wind from the Nadir become one way, the way the dead came in. Three lights with three owners mark it: the Sexton's green as far as he went, the Reckoner's warm lamp in his room, the sounder's cold lamps from the Pit. A next agent could have the sun's last ray grow by a few pieces at every gutter, or put a piece of the Nave's red in the jackdaw's nest, or bring the Reckoner's lamp up the Hypogeum's stair and on, far below, to this room.

## What the visitor never learns

Whether the Reckoner's stair below his door comes out here; why he lays it where nobody will see it shine; what he will do when the gold runs out.

## Open threads

- The sun's last ray is half laid. Does it grow, and with what gold, once the valley's suns are bare?
- The sounder's lamps stop at the room's door, as if they knew whose it was. Have the sounder and the Reckoner met?
- The wind that made the Flute comes from under the world through the Gulf. Where else does it come up?
