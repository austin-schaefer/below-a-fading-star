## The story

The Heliarchs, who faced the sun, took the valley's water under the south slope in a vaulted aqueduct to a round cistern-hall cut in the rock, and drew it off by a leat from the fen and a brook from the tarn into a mere at the slope's foot, where it goes under. What the cistern was for, nobody remembers.

The Noonwrights, who came after and tried to keep the sun instead of facing it, found the hall dry of light and full of water, and made it a garden. They cut no stone: they ran one of their lines in through the old aqueduct, laid along its floor under the water, and brought it up through the cistern's floor, and they hung a great lens in the crown of the dome and fed it stored noon from their lines, and under it, a measure at a time, they grew what the sun outside could no longer grow: grass that was green, fruit on trained trees, flowers whose colours had no names left in the valley. The water that comes down from the tarn and the fen runs in round the garden in stone rills and waters it.

Then the noon stopped coming. The line that fed the lens was shut at the far end (`the-man-at-the-wheel.md`), and the garden has been living since on what was left in the last length of tube, and the light under the lens has been shrinking. Every time it shrinks a ring of the garden dies, so the dead lies round the living in bands, oldest outermost, like the rings of a tree cut through: grey stalks, then brown, then wilting, then green. In the middle a circle of grass and three trees still stand in white noon, and the visitor can stand in it.

Somebody still waters it. A can stands at the edge of the light, full, as tall as a man, the same make as the tender's oil can a hundred metres up over the east moor.

## What it ties together

Where the valley's water goes; what the Noonwrights' stored noon was for, besides lamps (to grow things the dying sun could not); why their lines stopped (a shut valve, told at the other end); and the tender's other round. A next agent can give the garden's last trees fruit a visitor can take (an act: carry a fruit up into the red light and watch its colour change), bring the can's owner's footprints out of the water's edge, or open the shaft over the dome to the moor.

## What the visitor never learns

What the Heliarchs' cistern was for; how much light is left in the last length; who waters it.

## Open threads

- The can is the tender's make. Is the tender the waterer, or was it given?
- The rings could be counted. Does each ring match a gutter, or a measure drawn off by someone up the line?
- The tube that fed the lens runs west through the rock toward the sphere on the west wall's foot (`the-man-at-the-wheel.md`). Does it still hold a little noon between?
