## The story

The Elder builders cut the sign of the sun into a slab beside their road: a V, and a seat at its bottom for a disc they never set there. Long after, the Late folk, who fear whatever walks the moor at night and cannot leave an empty thing alone, put a painted arch on the slab's shoulders and hung from it, on chains, a lantern as big as a room, exactly where the disc should be, with a straw bed inside. A traveller who reaches it at nightfall takes off his clogs, sits in the slung seat at the foot, hauls himself up on the rope, climbs in through the hatch and sleeps by the lamp. Everyone who has slept there chalks a stroke on the slab, and anyone who stops may add theirs.

Tonight someone is up there, and he sleeps the way a big man sleeps in a small bed: the hatch open for air, his head in a red nightcap on the pillow at the sill, his quilt half out, one arm and one striped leg hanging into the night ten metres over the moor. His bindle is up there with him, leaning on the slab's shoulder by his head: left at the foot, it would be gone by morning. His other stocking dries on the winch below. When the sun gutters the lamp inside turns up.

## What it ties together

The Elder slab, the Late folk's arch and lamp, and the sign of the sun in its notch made into a bed: one site with two hands on it. The shoes left side by side answer the traveller's boots on the Span: here the shoes mean sleep.

## What the visitor never learns

Who he is. His face is never seen closer than ten metres.

## Open threads

- He could be the pedlar whose sack split, sleeping off the loss, or the man who lost a slipper, on his way back from the house.
- Someone keeps the halt: the lamp's oil and the arch's paint are fresh. The Leerie keeps a slate for lamp oil at the house; it may be his.
- From the west the hatch cannot be seen; a second sleeper, or a lamp-keeper, could face that way.
