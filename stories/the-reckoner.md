## The story

When the sun first faltered, a polity long gone appointed a Reckoner to keep its account: to note every gutter, against the day it would not come back. They cut the Hypogeum for him, a stair down to a bronze door, and he went in with his lamp and his tables and had the door closed behind him. The stair goes on far below the door. On the lintel outside he keeps his count where anyone may read it: eleven glyphs, and at every gutter he turns the row one place from within.

He is not dead and not asleep, only far down and busy. Lift the great ring and let it fall, slowly, three times, and he answers: from the depths the first time, nearer the next, just behind the bronze from the third. He brings his lamp up the stair; you can see it in the seam. He reads his row to you, glyph by glyph, and turns it; the third time he runs it the whole way round, so you can see the count is kept. He puts his hand flat on the floor on his side, as you put yours on the crystal, and its shadow comes out into the light. Then he pushes a bronze plate out under the door for you: the door's last glyph, and his reckoning of you: a spiral of wire cut in it with a bronze bead strung on it for each visit. Pick it up and turn it over: on its back he has cut the lantern hanging in its notch, the Lantern Halt on the causeway, and beside it, on the same kind of wire, saffron beads for the strokes you have chalked on the Halt's slab; if you have chalked none, the empty socket for the first. He keeps that count too, from down there. Go and chalk one, come back and knock, and the new plate has it. Then he takes his lamp back down. The door does not open. It is not that kind of door.

The mother crystal drinks from the sun. Lay a hand on it and it pulls a gutter out of the sun before its time, and the Reckoner, far off, dutifully turns his row for a gutter that was not his to count. Then the crystal sends its light out over the moor in the ground, down his stair and under his door, and his glyphs glow its blue from then on, and so does his lamp. The second time, he sends something back along it: an ember of the sun's colour, which the crystal keeps.

## What it ties together

The gutter (the one shared event) with the lintel (which counts it), the crystal (which can force one) and the gold tesserae that turn up in the jackdaw's nest and the moles' heaps: they could all have come up from under the door. A next agent could have the plate's glyph answered somewhere (the Scriveners form the same one), or let him send up a gold tessera like the ones the jackdaw and the moles turn up.

## What the visitor never learns

His name, how far down the stair goes, what he eats, and what happens at the gutter that does not recover.

## Open threads

- The astronomer walks the world without a story: he may be the Reckoner's correspondent, or his successor in waiting.
- The tesserae are gold from a mosaic floor: the Sunken Court's sun lost its gold to the jackdaw. Two floors, or one?
- The lens mount's door, elsewhere, stands ajar and lit: someone did come out of somewhere.
